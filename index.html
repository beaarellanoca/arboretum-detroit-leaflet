Arboretum Detroit

Growing the Poletown East community through urban forestry

We are a nonprofit organization dedicated to building and maintaining an internationally recognized arboretum spanning the city and inspiring visitors with the love and stewardship of trees.

Our mission is to demonstrate reforesting Detroit's landscape with stands that exhibit the form and diversity of trees, educating about the cultural, ecological, aesthetic, and economic roles that trees play in our lives locally and globally.

Treetroit One and Beyond

Arboretum Detroit's restoration efforts began with Treetroit 1, a pilot project established on Earth Day in 2019 that transformed vacant land into a diverse community forest. What began as a single restoration site has since grown into a network of projects throughout Poletown East, each contributing to a larger vision of an interconnected urban arboretum.

Each project site is carefully designed to restore ecological function while creating welcoming public spaces. Through native tree and wildflower plantings, habitat restoration, soil improvement, and long-term stewardship, Arboretum Detroit is transforming underutilized land into thriving urban forests that support biodiversity, pollinators, birds, and healthier neighborhoods.

The Giant Sequoia Forest

A key initiative is the partnership with Archangel Ancient Tree Archive and Poletown East residents to create a Giant Sequoia Forest in Detroit, with MoKirby Park serving as Arboretum Detroit's most concentrated planting site.

This project highlights Arboretum Detroit's commitment to resilient forests, healthy soils, and community-driven ecological restoration.

Students and community members actively participate in planting Giant Sequoias, gaining hands-on experience in tree stewardship and ecological restoration. Through activities like these, Arboretum Detroit connects the community to the process of growing a future urban forest while inspiring the next generation of environmental stewards.

Community Engagement

Equally important is the community behind every project. Volunteers, neighborhood residents, students, and partner organizations work together to plant trees, restore habitat, care for sites, and participate in educational events throughout the year.

Each project represents not only environmental restoration, but also a shared commitment to reconnecting people with nature and strengthening the Poletown East community through stewardship.

Why Detroit?

During the early to mid-20th century, Detroit became known as the Motor City as the automobile industry expanded and shaped the city's economy, population growth, and urban development.

At its peak in 1950, Detroit's population reached approximately 1.8 million residents, making it one of the largest cities in the United States. In the decades that followed, deindustrialization, suburbanization, and the loss of manufacturing jobs contributed to substantial population decline.

Vacant land and the environmental impacts of industrial history remain visible throughout the city, while community-led initiatives continue working to restore neighborhoods and create a more resilient urban environment.

Poletown East Neighborhood

Poletown East has a long history rooted in Detroit's Polish and Kashubian immigrant communities. Beginning in the 1870s, Polish immigrants settled in the area, which eventually became a center of Polish life in Detroit.

The neighborhood experienced major changes in the late twentieth century when the construction of a General Motors assembly plant led to the demolition of much of the surrounding community. Despite strong opposition from residents, approximately 4,200 people were relocated and more than 1,300 homes, businesses, churches, and a hospital were demolished.

Today, the landscape still reflects the effects of this history, with vacant lots and abandoned properties scattered throughout the neighborhood. At the same time, community projects create new opportunities for restoration and renewal.

Identifying Priority Areas for Urban Tree Planting

Detroit continues to face the environmental and economic impacts of industrial decline, including population loss, vacant land, and long-term disinvestment across many neighborhoods.

Vacant land accounts for a substantial share of total land coverage in some Detroit neighborhoods. These conditions create both challenges and opportunities for urban greening, habitat restoration, and community forestry.

Environmental Inequity

Across Detroit, patterns of heat exposure and socioeconomic vulnerability often overlap. By examining land surface temperature alongside demographic conditions, the analysis shows where environmental burdens may be concentrated.

Access to parks and green spaces is also uneven across the city. Larger parks and green spaces are more commonly located toward the outskirts of Detroit, while some neighborhoods face limited access to environmental resources that can provide shade, cooling, recreation, and other community benefits.

These patterns demonstrate how environmental conditions and socioeconomic factors can intersect, creating greater burdens for communities that may already face economic and social challenges.

Arboretum Detroit is working to transform these spaces through urban forestry, habitat restoration, and community stewardship. By planting trees, restoring ecosystems, and creating spaces where people can connect with nature, the organization is helping address the lasting environmental impacts of disinvestment while building a healthier and more resilient future for Detroit.
